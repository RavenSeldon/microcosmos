# THE DARK FOREST

DESIGN LOCK v0.1 · GAMEPLAY, VISUALS & DEVELOPMENT ROADMAP

In a forest where every light reveals a presence, what is the cost of being seen?

The Dark Forest is the stronger name. I would retire The Seventh Signal as the title, preserve its best mechanics, and make one significant change: the player must be vulnerable, and must be capable of striking back.

Not another research observatory. Not a civilization simulator disguised as a game. A beautiful, small, replayable strategy game about incomplete information, imperfect trust, survival, and the temptation to strike first.

## 2. The black dialectic

Thesis: A dark forest with hidden civilizations, glowing communications, and the ability to attack creates exciting strategic uncertainty.

Antithesis: If everyone is inscrutable and attacks are arbitrary, there is no actual strategy. If attacking first is always safest, the game becomes repetitive. If being peaceful always wins, it becomes a morality lesson rather than a game.

Synthesis: Make the world partially observable, but learnable. Players can infer behaviour without attaining certainty. Attacking must sometimes be necessary, sometimes disastrous, and sometimes an understandable risk that fails.

This leads to a simple design principle:

Four actions. Six rounds. Six unknown districts. One shared fate.

Everything else exists to support that experience.

## 1 per turn

Your role: You operate a small listening station positioned on a shared resource network. Six autonomous districts draw from that network, but their internal policies, needs, and intentions are concealed.

Beneath them lies the Seventh Basin, dependent on what remains. Your station can observe, communicate, hide, and forcibly interrupt another district's extraction. It cannot simply command the others.

Your objective: Survive six rounds while keeping the Seventh Basin from collapsing.

This is a fictional rule system, not a representation of the actual Seventh Seat experiment.

### Four buttons. That's it.

| Action       | Immediate effect                                                     | Strategic cost                                                |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| &#x20;LISTEN | Learn one reliable fact about a selected district's recent behaviour | Spend your turn investigating rather than intervening         |
| &#x20;SIGNAL | Contact a district and ask it to restrain extraction or cooperate    | Expose your position; the district may refuse                 |
| &#x20;STRIKE | Interrupt one district's extraction for this round                   | Reveal yourself and risk retaliation                          |
| &#x20;HIDE   | Reduce exposure and make yourself harder to attack                   | Gain no new information and leave others' decisions unchanged |

I especially want STRIKE to be real. Not merely a decorative animation or a warning with an aggressive name.

It must change the simulation immediately, with an actual tactical benefit under some conditions. But attacking a cooperative district can create new enemies, destroy a valuable relationship, and make the next round more dangerous.

Likewise, SIGNAL must sometimes achieve something a strike cannot: persuade a reciprocal district to restrain future withdrawals. And HIDE must be a legitimate strategic choice, not a joke button.

No additional stamina, research points, currencies, technology trees, or upgrade shops. One action per round is the attention budget.

### The black boxes

Each district has a concealed, deterministic decision policy. For the first version, three policy families are sufficient:

- Reciprocal: Can restrain extraction after constructive contact, but reacts negatively to attacks.
- Extractive: Prioritizes its local resources and rarely accepts costly cooperation.
- Preemptive: Interprets exposure and threatening behaviour as reasons to strike.

A district's responses also depend on its resource situation and interaction history. The player sees signals and past actions, never a magical indicator that instantly declares a node good or evil.

A critical fairness requirement: the player can always identify which district they are targeting. The uncertainty concerns its intentions, not whether the interface randomly sends the attack somewhere else.

The six districts commit their own decisions under hidden rules. Their observable activity appears, the player chooses one action, and the simulation resolves the consequences.

## 4. Visual Design Lock — the five references

This is where I would spend disproportionate care. The five images should not be flattened into a generic sci-fi backdrop.

Each has a different job.

Reference 1 — The celestial ring

The primary composition. A giant, intricate, circular instrument in a black void, with dense fine filaments and six spatially distinct node locations. It should feel like an astronomical observatory and a neural system simultaneously.

Reference 2 — The luminous nerves

The language of action. Hair-thin branching filaments that illuminate as signals propagate, intensify at contact, and break under attack. Their motion should trace actual network events, not decorative randomness.

Reference 3 — Obsidian topography

The physical ground beneath the orbital network. Black, dimensional terrain with extremely thin gilt contour lines. It gives the shared resource a material presence without making a second, competing dashboard.

Reference 4 — Nebular splendour

Atmospheric depth. The luminous dust, diffraction spikes, and muted reddish-blue gas create vastness. This should be restrained and mostly static during play, blooming into visibility at key narrative moments.

Reference 5 — The cosmic axis

The hero composition and opening reveal. A suspended central mechanism, radial architecture, and a delicate vertical connection descending toward the vulnerable seventh basin below.

HIDE

These animations should remain visible long enough to understand, but never delay the next move unnecessarily.

## 5. Lightweight by design

The visual ambition is high, but the implementation need not be heavy.

I recommend Three.js + TypeScript + Vite, with native HTML/CSS for the interface, and a small deterministic simulation engine independent of the renderer.

The technical constraints:

- Use a procedurally constructed orbital ring, instanced stars, and reusable curve geometry.
- Animate signal particles along predetermined paths, not through expensive physical simulations.
- Use a compressed original nebula texture or lightweight procedural background rather than volumetric gas rendering.
- Generate the terrain contour lines from a small fixed heightfield.
- Limit camera movement to a comfortable orbit, slight tilt, and controlled zoom.
- Offer reduced motion, keyboard-accessible actions, and a clear 2D fallback.
- Keep the simulation local: no login, database, real-time server, or inference API.

The target should be approximately 60 FPS on a representative desktop and at least 30 FPS on a representative midrange mobile device, with the initial compressed download ideally under 3 MB. These are acceptance targets to test, not performance claims.

Crucially, scene rendering must never change the game state. All state transitions should be reproducible with the same seed and actions.

## 6. The development roadmap

1. Stage 0 — Canon and architecture lock

   Establish the standalone `/dark-forest/` destination, inspect its actual deployment pipeline, document existing-site boundaries, and freeze the scope at four actions, six districts, and six rounds.

   Checkpoint G0: Written architecture and design contract approved; existing project routes untouched.
2. Stage 1 — The invisible game

   Build a headless deterministic simulator with six independently acting districts, resource accounting, player visibility, offensive attacks, and six-round resolution. Test it before adding 3D.

   Checkpoint G1: All actions have mechanically meaningful effects; identical seeds and choices reproduce identical outcomes; no impossible resource totals.
3. Stage 2 — The visual instrument

   Construct the ring, central axis, dark terrain, communication paths, and celestial backdrop. Implement the four signature action animations against scripted sample events.

   Checkpoint G2: Actual browser renders satisfy the five-reference art direction at desktop and mobile sizes. No concept poster counts as a passed production render.
4. Stage 3 — First playable vertical slice

   Connect the simulation to the visuals and interface. Add district targeting, four action controls, a compact status display, sound-off-by-default feedback, and an end-of-run reveal.

   Checkpoint G3: A new player can understand the objective and act without reading a technical manual. Every visible consequence corresponds to a simulation event.
5. Stage 4 — Adversarial playtesting

   Test seeded scenarios designed to challenge the mechanics: cases where striking helps, where striking hurts, where cooperation succeeds, and where hiding is strategically sound. Check that visual information never gives away hidden policies.

   Checkpoint G4: More than one viable strategy exists, the causes of wins and losses are inspectable, and victory is not predetermined by the designer's moral preferences.
6. Stage 5 — Performance, polish, and deployment

   Profile desktop and mobile performance, finish accessibility and fallback behavior, verify asset permissions, test the production build, and deploy the standalone route. Add cross-links only after acceptance.

   Checkpoint G5: Public URL loads cleanly, complete games work across supported devices, the existing sites remain unchanged, and all required quality gates pass.

## 7. What not to build yet

The first release does not need multiplayer, generative AI adversaries, diplomacy menus, customizable ship weapons, procedural universes, a campaign, or a dozen factions.

It also does not need a full counterfactual visualization engine. The initial end-of-run reveal and deterministic replay are sufficient. A more sophisticated comparison of alternate choices can follow if the core game is compelling.

Those exclusions protect what matters: the world must look extraordinary, the rules must be understandable, and choices must actually change what happens.

## The final design thesis

THE DARK FOREST / CANON CANDIDATE

# Silence is safety. Until it isn't.

Six hidden districts. One vulnerable commons. A finite number of decisions.

Read the signs. Reveal yourself. Strike first. Or remain unseen.

Every action costs an opportunity, and not every light in the darkness is an enemy.

How much can you know before you must act?

\#如實 · #無為 · #PLAY · 不二

My recommendation is to freeze this as Design Lock v0.1 and execute Stages 0 and 1 before investing heavily in production art, while developing the visual instrument as a parallel, independently reviewed gate.

That gives the project two non-negotiable standards: it must be an engaging game even without the spectacle, and its actual rendered spectacle must be worthy of the references.

The Dark Forest should not be an explanation of the research with a few buttons attached. It should be a world that makes the underlying dilemma genuinely playable.
